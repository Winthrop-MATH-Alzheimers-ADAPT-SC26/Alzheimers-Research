\section{Introduction}
Alzheimer's Disease (AD) is characterized as a complex neurodegenerative disease affecting more than 55 million people globally \cite{maji2025mathematical}. Symptoms are caused by the degeneration of the central nervous system, and are manifested by progressive memory loss, difficulty with language, drastic mood swings, among other effects. It is a disease that causes an extensive burden to those suffering from the disease through disorientation, to relatives in the form of mental taxation, as well as economically, projecting an estimated global economic burden of $16.9$ trillion dollars by 2050 \cite{nandi2022global}. These factors are just a few reasons as to the importance of research in this area, where many scientists have worked to understand the underlying mechanisms of AD. However, much is still unknown in how AD is caused, as well as what biological processes occur in the brain, making it difficult to come to a decisive conclusion regarding its cause.

Many studies have been conducted in regard to understanding Alzheimer's Disease. With this, there are a few main hypotheses that are generally considered to be factors in the causation and progression of AD \cite{zhang2024recent}. The so-called \textit{Amyloid Cascade Hypothesis} is among the most extensively studied in the literature, where it is proposed that a misfolding of $\beta$-amyloid ($A_\beta$) protein forms senile plaques. These plaques are hypothesized to be the main driving force in the intracellular deposition of misfolded $\tau$ proteins in neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) and memory loss in the progression of Alzheimer's Disease \cite{hardy1992alzheimer}. Growing evidence, however, shows that this hypothesis is suggested to be but a small factor in the larger picture of the underlying operative mechanisms of the disease \cite{kepp2023amyloid}. This has led researchers to branch into other pathways concerning AD, looking at the interactions between A$\beta$ and $\tau$ in an attempt to further understand the pathogenesis of these two factors \cite{zhang2021interaction}. Some studies have also observed the role of calcium in this process, further suggesting a more complex process that is occurring biologically. This occurs through a feedback interaction between an upregulation of Ca$^{2+}$ and the amyloidogenic pathway, remodeling the Ca$^{2+}$ signaling pathway \cite{berridge2010calcium}.

The various complex interactions in AD have shown to be an obstacle to yet overcome when modeling the behavior of the disease. Numerous different studies have in turn given an attempt at creating mathematical models observing different aspects of the disease in order to observe the underlying dynamics. This has led to many models being introduced, each aiming to understand various components in Alzheimer's Disease through different methodologies. Compartments concerning the interaction between $A_\beta$ and $\tau$ are studied, observing the suspected effects on neurodegeneration and cognitive decline through a mechanistic causal model \cite{petrella2019computational,hao2022optimal}. The feedback loop between $A_\beta$ and Ca$^{2+}$ has also been analyzed using a deterministic system, adding a noise-inducing element to the system \cite{pal2025nonequilibrium,zhang2020mathematical}. Models that observe lower-level processes have also been studied, lending to valuable insight as to what is suggested to be seen on the cellular level \cite{maji2026quantifying,lang2023stationary}.

It should be noted that in these models, while each has its own way of observing some of the underlying mechanisms, there are still potential shortfalls when it comes to finding effective treatments. Evidence suggests that focusing solely on anti-$A_\beta$ treatments or $\tau$ protein treatments, rather than attempting to determine treatments for many different aspects of the disease \cite{abdulkhaliq2026amyloid,zhang2021interaction}. This provides difficulty in understanding exactly the importance of which component of the disease should be the focus of further study in order to provide evidence for treatments having higher efficacy. In this study, a mechanistic model is proposed, analyzing the interaction between $A_\beta$, Ca$^{2+}$, and $\tau$ proteins; observing the effects of this multifaceted interaction on neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline. Sensitivity analysis is performed in order to better understand the importance of each aspect of the model, and optimal control is used in order to determine the suggested avenues of effective treatment. \bigskip

\section{Proposed Model}
To understand the role calcium may have in the interaction of AD, a model is proposed using five biomarkers found to have interactions within the disease: hallmark biomarkers through amyloid beta and tau, an interactional biomarker through calcium, suggested to have a role in amyloid beta and tau protein accumulation. 
Also observed are neuronal loss biomarkers, and cognitive decline. 
The pathology of AD is suggested to start through a feedback loop between amyloid beta and calcium through a dysregulation of calcium; this excacerbates the aggregation of amyloid beta, forming plaques. 
These plaques then promotes the hyperphosphorylation of tau proteins, leading to neurodegeneration and subsequently cognitive decline. \bigskip

\subsection{Amyloid Beta and Calcium Equations}
Because amyloid beta and calcium are suggested to interact in such a way that a dysregulation of calcium causes an acceleration of amyloid beta accumulation \cite{pal2025nonequilibrium}, this is modeled mathematically as 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:abeta_dt}
    \frac{\mathrm dA _{\beta}}{\mathrm dt}=a _{1} + a _{2}\frac{Ca }{Ca+\sigma} - k _{1}A _{\beta}-u _{1}A _{\beta}\quad \text{with} \quad A _{\beta}(T _{0})=A _{0},
\end{equation}
where $A _{0}$ is the initial condition of $A_\beta$ at age $T _{0}$, and can vary on a per patient basis \cite{hao2022optimal}. The rate of $A_\beta$ growth increases at a linear rate $a_1$ and decreases proportionally to the population of $A_\beta$, as described by $k_1 A_\beta$. Here, $a_2 \frac{Ca}{Ca + \sigma}$ acts as the interaction of calcium on $A _{\beta}$ using a Hill equation of $n=1$. The removal rate can be modified by choosing different values for $u_1$ which represents the effect of treatment targeting $A_\beta$. The term $u_1$ can either be constant or be a function of time.

Similarly, an $A_\beta$ interaction is observed to have a role in the further dysregulation of calcium, and the equation for calcium is defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:calcium_dt}
    \frac{\mathrm dCa}{\mathrm dt}= b _{1}+b _{2}A _{\beta}-k _{2}Ca - u _{2}Ca\quad \text{with} \quad Ca(T _{0})=Ca _{0}.
\end{equation}
Here, calcium grows at a linear rate $b_1$, and the accumulation of $A_\beta$ is positive at a rate $b _{2}$. There is also a natural removal rate of calcium, $k _{2}$. Notice that there is also the option to administer treatment targeting calcium through the term $u_2$. \bigskip

\subsection{Tau Equation}
Many studies have suggested that the pathology of tau is initiated by amyloid beta accumulation, and have modeled this process mathematically \cite{zheng2002amyloid, hao2022optimal}. However, it has also been suggested that calcium dysregulation also has an effect inducing the production of tau proteins \cite{guan2021elevating}. This leads to a definition of the tau equation to be given by 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm d \tau}{\mathrm dt}=c _{1}+c _{2}A _{\beta}+c _{3}Ca-k _{3}\tau-u _{3}\tau\quad \text{with} \quad \tau(T _{0})=\tau _{0}.
    \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
It is noted here that only pathological tau is observed, where other studies have used compartments relating to non-amyloid dependent tau accumulation, or suspected non-Alzheimer pathology (SNAP) \cite{petrella2019computational, hao2022optimal}. This function is similar to Equation~\eqref{eq:abeta_dt} and Equation~\eqref{eq:calcium_dt} in that $\frac{\mathrm d\tau}{\mathrm dt}$ grows at a linear rate $d_1$ and decreases proportional to the population of $\tau$ through $k_3 \tau$. There is also the treatment term $u_3 \tau$. \bigskip

\subsection{Neurodegeneration Equation}
When tau is in a pathological state, it no longer binds to microtubules as intended; instead, the formation of NFTs occur in the neurons, leading to neurodegeneration \cite{ballatore2007tau}. Hence, this equation is defined as 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\mathrm dN}{\mathrm dt}=d _{1}+d _{2}\tau-k _{4}N.
\end{equation*} \bigskip

This equation follows the established structure and also includes the interaction term $d_2 \tau$. \bigskip

\subsection{Cognitive Decline Equation}
Both tau and neurodegeneration are evidenced to directly affect cognitive decline \cite{bejanin2017tau, hao2022optimal}. Because of this, the equation for cognitive decline is defined as 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\mathrm dC}{\mathrm dt}=e _{1}+e _{2}NR + e _{3}\tau-k _{5}C.
\end{equation*}
The parameter $R$ represents a patient having the APOE $\epsilon 4$ gene, which has been shown to be a risk factor in Alzheimer's disease (need to find citation of this).

This model is summarized as a system of ordinary system of differential equations with 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:odesystem}
\begin{cases}
    \frac{\mathrm dA _{\beta}}{\mathrm dt}=a _{1} + a _{2}\frac{Ca }{Ca+\sigma} - k _{1}A _{\beta}-u _{1}A _{\beta}\\

    \frac{\mathrm dCa}{\mathrm dt}= b _{1}+b _{2}A _{\beta}-k _{2}Ca - u _{2}Ca\\

    \frac{\mathrm d \tau}{\mathrm dt}=c _{1}+c _{2}A _{\beta}+c _{3}Ca-k _{3}\tau-u _{3}\tau\\

    \frac{\mathrm dN}{\mathrm dt}=d _{1}+d _{2}\tau-k _{4}N\\

    \frac{\mathrm dC}{\mathrm dt}=e _{1}+e _{2}NR + e _{3}\tau-k _{5}C
\end{cases}
\quad
\text{with}
\quad
\begin{cases}
    A _{\beta}(T _{0})=A _{0}\\
    Ca(T _{0})=Ca _{0}\\
    \tau(T _{0})=\tau _{0}\\
    N(T _{0})=N _{0}\\
    C(T _{0})=C _{0}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation} \bigskip

\section{Analysis}
\subsection{Preliminary Results}
Assuming \eqref{eq:odesystem} is subject to initial conditions that are non-negative, it is noted that the system is invariant in the non-negative orthant. Let $r_1 = k_1 + u_1$ and $r_2 = k_2 + u_2$ for the simplicity of calculations. Considering the components uncoupled from the rest of the proposed model, $\mathrm dA _{\beta}/\mathrm dt,\mathrm dCa/\mathrm dt$ can be shown to be bounded. This is done by first noticing

\begin{equation*}
    a_1 + a_2\frac{Ca}{Ca + \sigma} - r_1 A_\beta \leq a_1 + a_2 - r_1 A_\beta
\end{equation*}

since $Ca/(Ca+\sigma)\in [0,1)$. 
So, $a_1 + a_2 - r_1 A_{\beta} < 0$ when $A_{\beta} > \frac{a_1 + a_2}{r_1}$. Similarly, using that $A _{\beta}$ is bounded, it follows that 

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\mathrm dCa}{\mathrm dt} = b_1 + b_2 A_{\beta} - r_2 Ca \leq b_1 + b_2 M - r_2 Ca
\end{equation*}

as $M > A_{\beta} (t)$ for all $t \geq 0$. So, $b_1 + b_2 M - r_2 Ca < 0$ when $Ca > \frac{b_1 + b_2 M}{r_2}$, thus $Ca$ is also bounded. The rest of the system follows from these two variables, so the rest of the system is bounded as well. \bigskip

\begin{lem}[Establishment of Unique Equilibria of Uncoupled System]\label{lem1}
    There exists a unique equilibria ($A _{\beta}^{*}, Ca ^{*}$) for the system
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:submodel}
        \begin{cases}
            \frac{\mathrm dA_{\beta}}{\mathrm dt} = a_{1} + a_{2} \frac{Ca}{Ca + \sigma} - r_1 A_{\beta} \\
            \frac{\mathrm dCa}{\mathrm dt} = b_{1} + b_{2} A_{\beta} - r_2 Ca. \\
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
    To establish the existence of equilibria for Equation~\eqref{eq:submodel}, a solution to the following must be obtained:
    \begin{align*}
        a_{1} + a_{2} \frac{Ca}{Ca+\sigma} - r_1 A_{\beta} &= 0\\
        b_{1} + b_{2} A_{\beta} - r_2 Ca &= 0.
    \end{align*}

    By solving $\frac{\mathrm d A_{\beta}}{\mathrm dt} = a_1 + \frac{a_2 Ca}{Ca + \sigma} - r_1 A_{\beta}$ for $A_{\beta}$, we get
    \begin{equation*}
        A_{\beta} = \frac{a_1 Ca + a_2 Ca + a_1 \sigma}{r_1 (Ca + \sigma)}.
    \end{equation*}

    We then substitute this value into $\frac{\mathrm d Ca}{\mathrm dt} = b_1 + b_2 A_{\beta} - r_2 Ca$. The resulting expression is,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:prequadratic}
        b_1 - r_2 Ca + \frac{b_2 (a_1 Ca + a_2 Ca + a_1 \sigma)}{r_1 (Ca + \sigma)}.
    \end{equation}

    Setting \eqref{eq:prequadratic} equal to 0 and doing further simplification yields the quadratic,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:quadratic}
        -(r_1 r_2)Ca^2 + (a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_2 + b_1 r_1 - r_1 r_2 \sigma)Ca + a_1 b_2 \sigma + b_1 r_1 \sigma = 0.
    \end{equation}

    Notice that in \eqref{eq:quadratic} the quadratic term is always negative and the constant term is always positive. The linear term can either be positive or negative depending on the choice of parameters. Regardless of the parity of the linear term, there is only one sign change in the expression, so by Descartes' Rule of Signs, there is one positive real root to this quadratic. 
    Thus, there exists a unique positive equilibrium ($A _{\beta}^{*},Ca ^{*}$) for the system in Equation~\eqref{eq:submodel}.
\end{proof} \bigskip

\begin{lem}[Global Stability of Equilibrium]\label{lem2}
    The equilibrium ($A _{\beta}^{*},Ca ^{*}$) is globally asymptotically stable.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\mathbf{F} (A_{\beta}, Ca) = \left\langle a_1 + \frac{a_2 Ca}{Ca + \sigma} - r_1 A_{\beta}, \, b_1 + b_2 A_{\beta} - r_2 Ca \right\rangle$. Then, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = -r_1 - r_2$. Choose the function $\alpha (A_{\beta}, Ca) = 1$. So, $\nabla \cdot (\alpha \mathbf{F}) < 0$, therefore by the Bendixson-Dulac Theorem, there is no limit cycle in the system of ODEs. So, the equilibrium point must be globally stable in the non-negative orthant.
\end{proof} \bigskip 

\begin{thm}[Global Stability of Equilibrium of System]
    There exists a unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium ($A _{\beta}^{*},Ca ^{*}, \tau ^{*}, N ^{*},C ^{*}$).
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
    By Lemma~\ref{lem2}, there exists a unique globally asymptotic stable equilibrium ($A _{\beta}^{*},Ca ^{*}$). By solving \eqref{eq:tau} for $\tau$ we get the following equilibrium value:
    \begin{equation*}
        \tau ^{*}=\frac{c _{1}+c _{2}A _{\beta}^{*}+c _{3}Ca ^{*}}{k _{3}+u _{3}}.
    \end{equation*}

    By similar methods we find
    \begin{equation*}
        N^* = \frac{d_1 + d_2 \tau^*}{k_4}
    \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*}
        C^* = \frac{e_1 + e_2 N^*R + e_3 \tau^*}{k_5}.
    \end{equation*}

    Thus there is a unique globally asymptotic stable equilibrium solution for the system in Equation~\eqref{eq:odesystem}.
\end{proof} \bigskip

\section{Results}

Using the derived biological parameters in Table~\ref{tab:params1}, we run the simulation forward for 50 years with the initial conditions $(A_0, Ca_0, \tau_0, N_0, C_0)=(0,100,0,0,0)$. This simulation uses solve\_ivp from the SciPy package in Python. The results from the simulation are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:general}. Initially, $A_\beta$ and $Ca$ increase and drive each other's growth. This leads to a large growth of $\tau$ proteins, thus leading to neuron loss over time, and cognitive decline over an even longer time scale. The biomarker populations reach their equilibrium values relatively early, but it takes cognitive decline many years to reach its equilibrium value. \bigskip

\section{Optimal Control}

Optimal control is a method we can use to determine the optimal values of $u_n$ while tracking some cost function. In optimal control, we will let each $u_n$ be a function of time, $u_n(t)$, as it is not realistic to maintain a constant treatment over a long time-frame. Tracking a cost function is useful for our application as some drugs used to treat Alzheimer's Disease have been shown to induce strong side effects such as brain swelling and brain bleeding (citation needed). So, minimizing this cost function while maximizing treatments will let the medication have its strongest impact without crossing some side-effect threshold. \bigskip

\subsection{Objective Function}

The following equation is our objective function which tracks our cost over the time horizon of the treatment.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:objective}
    J(u_1, u_2, u_3) = \int_0^K A_1 N + A_2 C + A_3 u_1^2 + A_4 u_2^2 + A_5 u_3^2 \, \mathrm dt.
\end{equation} \bigskip

The goal of treatment is to minimize the equilibrium values $N$ and $C$ while also being mindful of the side effects from the drugs. The terms $A_n$ are the weights which determine how important each associated variable is in the optimization problem. The variables $u_n$ are the treatment inputs. \bigskip

\subsection{Admissible Controls}

\begin{equation*}
    \label{eq:admissible}
    u = \left\{ (u_1, u_2, u_3) \in L^\infty (0, K)^3 \mid (u_1, u_2, u_3) \in \left[ 0, u_1^{\max} \right], \left[ 0, u_2^{\max} \right], \left[ 0, u_3^{\max} \right] \, \forall \, t \in [0, K] \right\}
\end{equation*} \bigskip

Each term $u_n$ must be non-negative during the duration of the treatment. \bigskip

\subsection{Hamiltonian and Adjoint Equations}

\begin{equation*}
\label{eq:hamiltonian}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal H &= A_1 N + A_2 C + A_3 u_1^2 + A_4 u_2^2 + A_5 u_3^2 + \lambda_1 \left( a_1 + a_2 \frac{Ca}{Ca + \sigma} - k_1 A_\beta - u_1 A_\beta \right) \\
    &\quad + \lambda_2 \left( b_1 + b_2 A_\beta - k_2 Ca - u_2 Ca \right) + \lambda_3 \left( c_1 + c_2 A_\beta + c_3 Ca - k_3 \tau - u_3 \tau \right) \\
    &\quad + \lambda_4 \left( d_1 + d_2 \tau - k_4 N \right) + \lambda_5 \left( e_1 + e_2 NR + e_3 \tau - k_5 C \right) .
\end{split}
\end{equation*} \bigskip

The Hamiltonian includes the cost function as described in Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} as well as the co-state vectors for each variable present in our system. This leads to the adjoint equations:

\begin{equation*}
\label{eq:adjoints}
\begin{cases}
    \frac{\mathrm d\lambda_1}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{- \partial \mathcal H}{\partial A_\beta} =  k_1 \lambda_1 + u_1 \lambda_1 - b_2 \lambda_2 - c_2 \lambda_3 \\

    \frac{\mathrm d\lambda_2}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{- \partial \mathcal H}{\partial Ca} = -\lambda_1 \frac{a_2 \sigma}{\left( Ca + \sigma \right)^2 } + k_2 \lambda_2 + u_2 \lambda_2 - c_3 \lambda_3 \\

    \frac{\mathrm d\lambda_3}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{- \partial \mathcal H}{\partial \tau} = k_3 \lambda_3 + u_3 \lambda_3 - d_2 \lambda_4 - e_3 \lambda_5 \\

    \frac{\mathrm d\lambda_4}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{- \partial \mathcal H}{\partial N} = -A_1 + k_4 \lambda_4 - e_2 R \lambda_5 \\

    \frac{\mathrm d\lambda_5}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{- \partial \mathcal H}{\partial C} = -A_2 + k_5 \lambda_5
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}

with transversality conditions
\begin{equation*}
    \label{eq:transversality}
    \lambda_1 (K) = \lambda_2 (K) = \lambda_3 (K) = \lambda_4 (K) = \lambda_5(K) = 0.
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Characterizations}
\begin{equation*}
\label{eq:characterizations}
\begin{cases}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial u_1} = 2 A_3 u_1 - \lambda_1 A_\beta = 0, & u_1^* = \min \left\{ u_1^{\max}, \max \left\{ 0, \frac{\lambda_1 A_\beta}{2 A_3} \right\} \right\} \\

    \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial u_2} = 2 A_4 u_2 - \lambda_2 Ca = 0, & u_2^* = \min \left\{ u_2^{\max}, \max \left\{ 0, \frac{\lambda_2 Ca}{2 A_4} \right\} \right\} \\

    \frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial u_3} = 2 A_5 u_3 - \lambda_3 \tau = 0, & u_3^* = \min \left\{ u_3^{\max}, \max \left\{ 0, \frac{\lambda_3 \tau}{2 A_5} \right\} \right\}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation*} \bigskip
